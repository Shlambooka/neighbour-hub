Meeting 13: What is Privilege

Date: August 30, 2026

Participants: 15 (8 adults, 7 kids)

Notes:

  • Defining privilege: Living with no problems – you're not restricted, there's no negativity added to your life in comparison to others. It is the lack of need to recognize systemic oppression. People don't think about it because it doesn't impact them. Analogy: "If you're underwater, you are automatically aware that you cannot breathe; but if a fish is underwater, they don't have to think about it."
  • Privilege is tied to oppression, not just a benefit. Different types exist, e.g., racial (white privilege).
  • Avoid TERF Hortons while they support JK Rowling, who doesn't believe in trans women and spends money to take away their rights.
  • Intersectionality – recognizing how different forms of oppression overlap.
  • Miscegenation: Marrying across racial lines. In Brazil, this was intentionally used to delete the history of oppression – weaponized to erase history.
  • Economic privilege: The greatest predictor of whether you will be poor or not is the family you are born into. Social mobility – the ability to move up or down between classes – is limited. "It's expensive to be poor." The system gives money to those who need it less, making it harder for those who need it to rise. The government subsidizes rental companies but doesn't support those living in tents because it isn't a residence; the province doesn't help those who need it most. Having a house is a big privilege; renting sets you up to not be able to save for a house.
  • Entitlement example: Children taking grapes from a school garden – a good example of entitlement to take things and forgetting about others.
  • Passport privilege: A member noticed a clear shift in how they were treated when an officer saw they didn't have a Canadian passport. "If you're Canadian you're fine, if you're not you're a threat." Travel restrictions cause loss of relationships due to the hours spent going through customs.
  • Cisgender privilege: The privilege of having a cis child is assuming they are safe. LGBTQIA+ children have an extra layer of safety concerns (e.g., boys vs. girls sports teams and whether the child is safe in the bathroom). Language is the problem – the fact that we need to identify people by gender. A member noted: "I am cis but don't want to be" – they/them can be used instead.
  • Able-bodied privilege: The ability to go out (e.g., to Walmart) while some people cannot due to disabilities.
  • Car privilege: The ability to get up and go right where you want to go; time could be better spent at home instead of being in transit for hours.
  • Children and privilege: Children don't always understand privilege – they have a roof over their heads and food yet yearn for the latest trend (e.g., an electric scooter). "Anytime you see a decision that someone made and you think 'wow, that's a weird decision,' it's probably because they have no other choice."
  • Male privilege: Men can rest and relax in open settings (e.g., on the bus). Men can walk alone at night, while women are usually afraid (especially given there is currently a man exposing himself in the community). Men's voices are often centered in conversations (business or otherwise) over women's.
  • Social privilege: Extroverts are comfortable chatting with everyone, while introverts may not be.
  • Gender oppression: A historical power imbalance that disadvantages women.
  • Race oppression: Individuals of African Canadian/American descent do not have the same level of access to career mobility, are deemed more violent than white individuals, and entire groups do not have access to services (such as abortions) even though it is legal in Canada. A member recommended the article "Understanding Bodily Autonomy Through Triple Oppression."
  • Pretty privilege / skinny privilege: The ability to move up based on image and body. Rejecting pretty privilege means dismissing how we assign work based on physical appearance.
  • Animal privilege: Saying a dog is friendly while it exhibits aggressive behavior (barking or trying to lunge at others), and/or assuming everyone likes animals – owners can be offended when people don't. There is entitlement to take up space because you know your dog and assume everyone else knows they are a good dog. Reminder: this space is dog-free and peanut-free.
  • Children have less privilege: Adults often feel entitled to take a child's body, e.g., demanding a hug.
  • Age privilege: Older individuals disregarding the wishes of others, being unable to accept that someone else is saying no.
  • Ableism privilege: Not having to defend yourself when you need to defend your needs. Example: someone asks for something in writing and the other person repeats themselves verbally instead of honoring the written request.
  • Next meeting (weather permitting): September 6 – discuss Ladies' First on Netflix. Also discuss how companies like Amazon use infrastructure while not paying taxes to build it.
